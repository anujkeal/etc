# ETC RESEARCH PROTOCOL

## 1. Purpose

This document defines how to research a documentary before writing it. Research must be traceable, balanced, and sufficient to support the exact claims used in narration.

## 2. Source hierarchy

Prefer sources in this order, depending on the subject:

1. **Primary evidence:** original research papers, technical standards, patents, source code, official specifications, original historical documents, datasets, direct interviews, and first-hand records.
2. **Official documentation:** documentation from the project, institution, manufacturer, standards body, government agency, or person responsible for the system.
3. **Academic and institutional sources:** university publications, textbooks, peer-reviewed reviews, museums, archives, and research institutions.
4. **Established specialist journalism and books:** reputable reporting with named authors, editorial standards, and links to underlying evidence.
5. **Community discussions:** useful for discovering practical experiences and questions, but not sufficient alone for important factual claims.

Search snippets, AI-generated summaries, anonymous posts, and unsourced social-media claims are discovery leads, not final evidence.

## 3. Research procedure

1. Search the central question and its key subtopics.
2. Identify primary and authoritative sources.
3. Read the relevant source material; do not rely only on snippets.
4. Cross-check important claims using independent sources where practical.
5. Search for counterexamples, limitations, and competing explanations.
6. Record exact URLs, titles, authors or organisations, dates, and relevant sections.
7. Build a claim ledger.
8. Draft only after the evidence base is adequate.

## 4. Claim ledger

Use this table during research:

| ID | Exact proposed claim | Source and URL | Evidence location | Confidence | Qualification |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| C01 | Claim in precise wording | Source title + URL | Section/page | High/Medium/Low | Necessary context |

Confidence meanings:

- **High:** directly supported by strong primary or authoritative evidence.
- **Medium:** supported by credible sources but with limitations, indirect evidence, or some uncertainty.
- **Low:** weak, conflicting, incomplete, or not independently verified.

Low-confidence claims should normally be omitted or clearly qualified.

## 5. Rules for different evidence types

### Technical and scientific claims
- Prefer official specifications, standards, documentation, and original papers.
- Identify the version, implementation, architecture, or conditions when relevant.
- Do not turn a common implementation into a universal rule.
- Check units, definitions, assumptions, and boundary conditions.
- Distinguish theoretical possibility from real-world behaviour.

### Historical claims
- Prefer original documents, archives, institutional histories, and scholarly work.
- Verify dates, names, sequence of events, and attribution.
- Do not assign a complex invention to one person if the evidence shows multiple contributors.
- Distinguish the date an idea was proposed, demonstrated, published, standardised, and widely adopted.

### Statistics
- Record the original source, date, population, sample, geography, and methodology.
- Check whether the statistic is current enough for the claim.
- Do not compare numbers measured using different methods without explaining the difference.
- Avoid statistics that add drama but do not help explain the subject.

### Quotations
- Use exact wording only when the original source can be checked.
- Preserve context.
- Attribute accurately.
- If exact wording cannot be confirmed, paraphrase without quotation marks.

## 6. Analogies and simplifications

Analogies are teaching tools, not evidence.

For each analogy, record:
- What part of the mechanism it helps explain.
- Where the comparison stops matching reality.
- Whether the analogy could create a false belief.

Add a brief qualification in the narration whenever the limitation matters.

## 7. Citation and source integrity

- Every important factual claim must have a traceable supporting source.
- Put source references in the research notes and associate them with claim IDs.
- Include a source list in the final package.
- Never invent URLs, titles, authors, dates, quotations, page numbers, or access details.
- Do not cite a source merely because its title sounds relevant.
- Do not imply a source supports a claim it does not actually support.
- If a source is inaccessible, mark it as inaccessible and find another source.
- Prefer direct links to the exact documentation or paper, not just an organisation's homepage.

## 8. Contradictions and uncertainty

When credible sources disagree:

1. Identify exactly what they disagree about.
2. Check whether they use different definitions, versions, periods, or assumptions.
3. Look for newer primary evidence.
4. Explain the disagreement if it matters to the story.
5. Avoid presenting a disputed interpretation as settled fact.

Use wording such as "in this implementation", "typically", "under these conditions", or "the available evidence suggests" when appropriate.

## 9. Research completion test

Research is ready for scripting only when:

- The central question has a defensible answer.
- The core mechanism is understood.
- Important claims have supporting sources.
- Key terminology is checked.
- Important exceptions and limitations are known.
- The proposed story does not depend on an unsupported claim.
- A useful example or demonstration can be explained accurately.

If these conditions are not met, continue researching or narrow the subject.
